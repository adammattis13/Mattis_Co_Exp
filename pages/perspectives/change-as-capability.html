What Leaders Get Wrong About Large-Scale Change

The biggest transformation failures share a common thread: they treat change as a project, not a capability. Here's the difference—and why it matters.

Adam Mattis December 2024 6 min read

I've seen dozens of transformation programs launch with fanfare and stall within months. The pattern is remarkably consistent: executive commitment, consultant engagement, ambitious roadmaps, early wins... then friction, fatigue, and eventually quiet abandonment.

The strategies weren't wrong. The leaders weren't incompetent. The organizations weren't resistant. So what happened?

In almost every case, the answer is the same: they treated transformation as a project rather than a capability.

The Project Mindset

When leaders approach transformation as a project, they think in terms of beginnings and endings. There's a current state, a future state, and a program to bridge them. Success means reaching the future state. Then the work is done.

This framing feels natural. It's how we manage most work. But transformation doesn't work this way.

Markets keep shifting. Technology keeps evolving. Competitors keep adapting. The "future state" you designed becomes outdated before you reach it. And because you built a project rather than a capability, you lack the organizational muscle to adapt.

Transformation is not a destination—it's a way of operating. Organizations that thrive don't transform once; they build the capability to transform continuously.

The Capability Mindset

Organizations that succeed at transformation think differently. They ask: How do we build an organization that can change and keep changing?

This shifts the focus from roadmaps to muscles. Instead of planning the perfect sequence of changes, they build the organizational capabilities that make change possible:

Sensing capability. The ability to see changes in the environment early—shifts in customer needs, competitive moves, technology disruptions—and interpret their implications.

Decision capability. The ability to make strategic decisions quickly, with appropriate information but without analysis paralysis. This requires clear decision rights and leaders who are empowered to act.

Execution capability. The ability to translate decisions into action at scale. This requires aligned incentives, clear accountability, and processes that enable rather than constrain.

Learning capability. The ability to learn from both successes and failures, and to incorporate those lessons into future decisions. This requires psychological safety and systems for capturing and sharing knowledge.

What This Looks Like in Practice

Organizations with transformation capability don't launch "transformation programs." They operate in a continuous rhythm of sensing, deciding, executing, and learning.

They make many small changes rather than a few large ones. They experiment constantly. They fail fast and learn faster. They don't wait for perfect information—they act on what they know and adjust as they learn more.

This doesn't mean they lack strategic direction. Quite the opposite—having a clear strategic intent makes it possible to make fast decisions without endless deliberation. The strategy provides the "why"; the capability provides the "how."

Building the Muscle

If your organization treats change as a project, you can't flip a switch and suddenly have transformation capability. But you can start building the muscle:

Start with decision rights. Clarify who can decide what, at what level. Push decisions down to where information lives. Remove approval layers that add time without adding value.

Create feedback loops. Build systems that surface signals from customers, markets, and operations. Make these signals visible to decision-makers. Act on them quickly.

Normalize failure. Create space for experiments that might not work. Celebrate learning from failure as much as success. Punish hiding failure more than failure itself.

Practice small changes. Don't wait for the big transformation to build the muscle. Make small structural changes regularly. Each one builds organizational comfort with change.

The goal isn't to eliminate the need for transformation—it's to make transformation part of how you operate rather than something that happens to you.
