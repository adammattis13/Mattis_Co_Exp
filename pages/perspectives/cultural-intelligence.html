Cultural Intelligence in Global Transformation

Clear, direct communication can move mountains—or break trust. What works in New York fails in Tokyo. A lesson in cultural intelligence learned the hard way.

Adam Mattis January 2025 5 min read

In my years in the military, I learned a communication technique that served me well: clear, concise, direct. When life or property are at stake, precision matters. There's no room for ambiguity.

Since leaving the military, I've found this approach—delivered with calm and rational precision—effective in most business contexts. Especially in places like the Nordics and Germany, where clarity and precision are highly valued. In New York and Boston, my direct style is perfectly normal.

That is, until it's not.

When Clarity Becomes a Liability

Each culture has its own patterns for decision-making, exercising influence, and offering direction. When communicating with colleagues in Latin America, I've found that directness works well only after rapport and trust have been established. The relationship comes first; the business follows.

Recently, I ran into trouble in Asia. Sitting in the comfort of the US, needing to quickly course-correct with a colleague from another part of the world, I forgot about cultural nuance. I proceeded with professional precision until my point was made crystal clear.

By the time I was done, I knew I had screwed up.

No matter how experienced you are, you will make mistakes. What matters more than the mistake itself is how you recover once it's been made.

The Recovery

Recognizing how I made the other person feel, I immediately shifted to a culturally appropriate posture. I humbled myself and clarified my intent. Even with that, it took weeks of consistent, culturally aware communication to restore our productive rhythm.

I still feel awful about it.

The Lesson for Transformation Leaders

With leadership and influence comes responsibility—not only for outcomes, but for how we achieve them. When working with international teams, leading in a style well-received across cultures adds another axis of complexity to an already complex job.

Global transformations fail for many reasons. Technical challenges. Political resistance. Misaligned incentives. But sometimes they fail simply because leaders communicate in ways that break trust rather than build it.

The most effective transformation leaders I've worked with share a common trait: they're students of cultural context. They adapt their style to the situation. They know when directness serves and when it destroys.

A Practical Framework

Before critical communications with international colleagues, I now ask myself three questions:

What's the relationship context? Have we built enough trust for direct feedback? Or does the relationship need more investment first?

What's the cultural context? How does this culture typically handle disagreement, criticism, and correction? What signals respect in this context?

What's at stake if I get this wrong? A momentary efficiency gain is never worth a damaged relationship. The math rarely works in favor of speed over sensitivity.

I share this story because the greatest tragedy from any mistake is failing to learn from it—and missing the opportunity to help others learn from it too.
